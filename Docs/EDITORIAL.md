# Ananta editorial foundation

Ananta is a beginner's doorway into Hindu traditions. It combines original orientation with identified primary texts, source links, and a connected reference layer. It does not claim that Hinduism has a closed canon or that a finite course can teach everything.

## What this edition contains

- `knowledge.json`: 70 original introductory entries, each with a short summary, two explanatory sections, named traditions, alternate spellings, related entries, library links, and sources.
- `lessons.json`: 12 ordered introductory lessons, with 60 explanatory sections, linked concepts and texts, and one review question with an explanation per lesson.
- About 8,600 words across wiki summaries, wiki sections, and lesson sections; titles, metadata, and quiz explanations are additional.
- The library and its separately maintained ingestion documentation define which primary works are actually bundled, which are partial, and which have guides only. An entry linking to a work does not imply that its full text is included.

These introductions are original educational prose. They are not translations, revealed scripture, quotations attributed to deities, or replacements for specialist commentary. Lesson reading times include reflection and the review question rather than only mechanical reading speed.

## Core editorial commitments

1. Name the perspective. Use formulations such as “in Advaita,” “the Vishnu Purana presents,” and “many devotees” when a claim belongs to a school, text, or practice. Do not flatten Vaishnava, Shaiva, Shakta, Smarta, regional, philosophical, and household traditions into one creed.
2. Treat deities and revered figures as participants in living religious worlds. “Character” is convenient for navigation, but does not imply fictional status. The app neither establishes nor denies metaphysical claims by presenting a story.
3. Preserve meaningful disagreement. The identity of atman and Brahman is not interpreted uniformly. Divine relationships vary. A shared vocabulary does not imply interchangeable doctrines.
4. Distinguish Brahman, Brahma, and Brahmin; distinguish the Goddess Kali from Kali Yuga; distinguish Rama of Ayodhya from uses of Rama for Balarama; distinguish different figures named Bharata.
5. Separate traditional attribution, narrative setting, and modern history. Vyasa and Valmiki are traditional author figures. That status is different from evidence that one historical writer composed every layer of a surviving work.
6. Keep ethical inquiry open. Explain caste and gender prescriptions as historical, contested claims; do not treat them as a universal description of Hindus or a mandatory present-day rule. Do not use karma to blame people for suffering. Do not use disability as a moral diagnosis.
7. Represent women as questioners and agents. Maitreyi, Gargi, Sita, Draupadi, and Andal are substantive entries. The importance of Sanskrit should not erase Tamil and other regional religious literatures.
8. Distinguish empirical history from sacred cosmology. Puranic durations and creation narratives are presented as identified traditional models. A precise slider value does not make a theological account a scientific measurement.

## Learning sequence

The course starts with diversity and ways of asking questions, then maps textual genres. It introduces action and ethics before self, rebirth, and liberation. Divine forms receive a contextual introduction before the two epics and a focused reading of the Gita. Practice, philosophical disagreement, cosmic time, and lived religion complete the orientation.

Each lesson's question tests an interpretive distinction rather than requiring religious assent. Answers are zero-indexed in JSON. A learner can understand what a school teaches without personally endorsing it.

The initial four wiki categories are navigation conveniences. “Sage” also includes poet-saints and philosophical teachers. “Epic figure” currently includes narrative figures such as Mahishasura, whose principal source is Puranic rather than an epic. Sita, Hanuman, and Rama are categorized as deities because they are worshipped; Draupadi and Andal also have divine significance in particular communities, which their articles make explicit. Future taxonomy should permit multiple roles and a general “Narrative figure” category.

## Source and rights practice

Every article and lesson carries references with source titles and URLs. Reference editions need not be identical to the edition bundled in the reader. For example, some wiki notes use Dutt's clearly identified abridgments of epic episodes, while the library may carry Griffith or Ganguli. A reference to an episode is not a claim that an abridgment is a complete original text.

Sources used for original paraphrases include:

- [Harvard's Pluralism Project](https://pluralism.org/introduction-to-hinduism), for diversity, textual authority, devotion, ritual, temples, and lived practice.
- [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/hindu-ph/), including its specialist entries on Yoga, Samkhya, Advaita, Ramanuja, and Madhva.
- [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/hinduism-and-hindu-art), [Rubin Museum](https://rubinmuseum.org/highlights/hindu-gods-and-goddesses/), and identified British Museum records for particular images and their regional settings.
- [Archana Venkatesan's UC Davis publication page](https://archana.faculty.ucdavis.edu/translations/andal-tiruppavai/) and the [Sri Vaishnava Tiruppavai resource](https://www.ramanuja.org/sv/alvars/andal/tiruppavai.html) for Andal and living interpretation.
- [IIT Kanpur's Tulsidas introduction](https://www.ramcharitmanas.iitk.ac.in/content/tulsidas) and [Oxford's Kavitavali editorial project](https://tulsidas.orient.ox.ac.uk/) for Tulsidas and textual transmission.
- Identified older translations of the Rig Veda, Upanishads, Vishnu Purana, Devi Mahatmya, Ramayana, Mahabharata, and Yoga Sutras, linked to the actual edition or passage.

Modern translations and museum photographs have not been copied into these two JSON resources. References to modern works do not grant a license to redistribute them. Original prose, a public-domain underlying Sanskrit work, a modern translation, a scan, and a photograph are separate rights questions. The current Sanskrit/name field contains names and transliterations; it is not a Sanskrit-text corpus. Andal's name is shown in Tamil and labeled accordingly.

Older translations can contain antiquated language, interpretive choices, omissions, and colonial-era assumptions. Keep their translator and scope visible. Treat bibliographic metadata and source-site editorial prose separately from the underlying primary text. Source links were researched during development on October 4, 2026; external pages can subsequently change.

## Expansion and review

A production content process should assign stable passage and entity identifiers, document source edition and scope, add reliable transliteration and language metadata, and record changes when an interpretation is revised. New links must resolve before release. A search hit should retain its source and passage location.

The next editorial expansion should deepen regional and lived traditions, ritual contexts, philosophical schools beyond Vedanta, further women and marginalized voices, and sources in Indian languages. Knowledge breadth must grow through sourced entries rather than a promise of exhaustive coverage.

These resources have been checked for schema shape, duplicate IDs, valid internal references, nonempty sources, and quiz answer bounds. They have not undergone independent expert review. Before broad publication, invite scholars and practitioners from several traditions to review factual accuracy, representational balance, and the tone of descriptions. That is a future publishing quality process, not a claim already completed by this development pass.
